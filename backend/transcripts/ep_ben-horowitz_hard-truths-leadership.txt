Episode: Hard Truths — Why Founders Fail and Why You Need to Run Toward Fear
Guest: Ben Horowitz (co-founder, a16z)
Host: Lenny Rachitsky
Publish Date: 2025-09-11
Source: Lenny's Podcast (ChatPRD/lennys-podcast-transcripts, public archive)

---

Ben Horowitz: The worst thing you do as a leader is hesitate on the next decision. The thing that causes you to hesitate is that both decisions are horrible. One of my bigger ones was we went public with $2 million in trailing 12 months revenue at 18 months old. That's obviously a bad idea. But the alternative was going bankrupt, and that's a worse idea.

Lenny: It's very difficult and painful to be a CEO, to be a founder. In spite of that, so many people want to start companies.

Ben Horowitz: The psychological muscle you have to build to be a great leader is to be able to stare into the abyss and go, "Okay, that way's slightly better. We're going to go that way." If everybody agrees with the decision, then you didn't add any value — they would've done that without you. The only value you ever add is when you make a decision that most people don't like.

Lenny: You wrote one of the most famous pieces for product managers — Good Product Manager, Bad Product Manager.

Ben Horowitz: What I was trying to get across is that the PM job is fundamentally a leadership job. And it's a tricky leadership job because nobody is actually reporting to you. It doesn't matter if you write a good spec or you have a good interview. What matters is that the product works. The PM is the mini CEO of the product — they own the outcome, not just the process.

Ben Horowitz on hiring:

The most common hiring mistake founders make is optimizing for experience instead of the problem they're actually trying to solve. "This person has done this exact thing before" sounds reassuring, but the startup you're building has never existed before. You need someone who can adapt to ambiguity, not someone who's comfortable executing a known playbook.

The second mistake: hiring someone to solve a problem you don't want to deal with yourself. If you're the CEO and you hate sales, you hire a VP of Sales to avoid sales. That almost never works. You have to understand the function well enough to evaluate whether the person is doing a good job. If you can't evaluate them, you can't manage them.

Ben Horowitz on culture:

Culture is the operating system of your company. When you're small, you can coordinate through conversations. When you scale, people make thousands of decisions without you in the room. Culture is the set of values and behaviors that determine how those decisions get made. If you don't design your culture deliberately, you get a default culture — and the default is almost always the accumulated habits of the first 20 people you hired.

What you do matters more than what you say. If you tell people integrity matters and then you keep a high performer who behaves unethically, you've just told everyone that integrity doesn't actually matter. The organization watches what you tolerate.

Ben Horowitz on the hardest parts of being a CEO:

People management is the hardest thing. Not strategy, not fundraising, not building the product. Managing people — specifically, having the conversations you don't want to have. Most founders avoid conflict. They give vague feedback because direct feedback feels harsh. But vague feedback is actually crueler, because the person doesn't know what to change. Be direct. Be specific. Tell people exactly what you need from them.

The other hard thing: you have to believe things other people don't believe yet, and you have to maintain that belief under enormous pressure. Every investor who says no, every big deal you lose, every competitor who seems to be winning — all of it is evidence against your thesis. Your job is to hold the thesis while still being genuinely open to information that should change it. That's a very narrow needle to thread.

Ben Horowitz on building product:

Good PMs know what they want. They've internalized the user so deeply that when they walk into a design review, they don't need to be shown 15 options. They know which direction is right. The best PMs I've seen operate like good editors — they can look at a product and immediately feel what's wrong, even before they can articulate why. That sensibility is what separates great PMs from good ones.

The product is the truth. You can have a great strategy deck, a brilliant roadmap, a perfectly structured team. But the user is in front of the product, not your documents. When something isn't working, the answer is almost always in the product itself — in the onboarding, the core flow, the moment of value delivery. That's where the work is.

Key takeaways:
- Your value as a leader comes from making unpopular decisions — not easy consensus calls
- The PM role is fundamentally a leadership role; you own the outcome, not the process
- Hire for the problem you're trying to solve, not for the resume that sounds safe
- Culture is the operating system of your company — design it deliberately or accept the default
- What you tolerate defines your culture more than what you say you value
- Direct feedback is kinder than vague feedback — people need to know exactly what to change
- Great PMs develop a sensibility for what's wrong in a product before they can articulate why
- The product is the truth — the answer to what's not working is always in the product itself
